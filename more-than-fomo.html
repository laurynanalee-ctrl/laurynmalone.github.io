More Than FOMO

The Fear of Being Replaceable

August 2026

There are a lot of things people tell you about having babies.

They tell you about the sleepless nights.

The newborn fog.

The way your entire life rearranges itself around this tiny person you love more than you knew was possible.

But by the time I had my third baby, I thought I knew what to expect.

I'd done this before.

I knew there would be hard nights and beautiful mornings. I knew there would be days when getting everyone dressed felt like an accomplishment. I knew my hands would be fuller, my schedule would be tighter, and spontaneity would become even harder.

What I didn't expect was how much my friendships would change too.

I expected another baby to make my house fuller. I didn't expect my circle to get smaller.

It wasn't some huge falling-out.

There wasn't a fight.

There wasn't a conversation.

No one told me they didn't want me around anymore.

It just... happened.

The invitations became fewer.
Plans happened without me.
Pictures would pop up afterward, and I'd realize people had gotten together and I hadn't even known about it.

And yes, there was FOMO.

I'd be lying if I said there wasn't.

But eventually I realized what I was feeling was about so much more than missing out.

I didn't just want to be invited. I wanted to know I was still someone people thought to invite.

At first, I brushed those moments off.

They probably thought I was busy.

They probably assumed I couldn't come.

It's not a big deal.

But one time becomes another.

New routines form. New friendships grow. People get used to making plans without you.

And eventually, what still feels strange to you becomes normal to everyone else.

Sometimes one person is grieving the loss of a friendship while the other person doesn't even realize anything has been lost.

And I genuinely don't believe anyone was trying to hurt me.

I don't think anyone woke up one morning and decided, Let's stop inviting her.

That's almost what makes it harder to explain.

There wasn't necessarily anyone to blame.

Life just kept moving.

I had three kids now. My hands were full. My life looked different.

Maybe they thought I'd say no.
Maybe they thought bringing the kids would be too difficult.
Maybe they assumed someone else had checked on me.
Maybe they thought, She's busy. She's fine.

Meanwhile, I was quietly wondering why no one ever asked.

The hardest part wasn't missing the plans. It was realizing no one seemed to notice I was missing from them.

And it happened during a season when I probably needed friendship more than ever.

Yes, this was my third baby.

I knew how to change diapers. I knew how to survive on very little sleep. I knew babies eventually grow out of the stage where your entire day revolves around feeding and naps.

But knowing how to care for a baby doesn't mean you don't need someone to care about you.

I was still adjusting.

Still exhausted.

Still trying to balance being a wife, a mom to three little boys, and somehow still being me underneath all of it.

And somewhere between becoming a mom of three and everyone assuming my hands were full, my circle got smaller too.

That left me with questions I didn't really know what to do with.

Did I do something wrong?

Did having another baby make me harder to be friends with?

Did everyone assume I didn't have room for friendship anymore?

Was I actually too busy—or did people simply stop asking?

If I could be left out that easily, had I ever mattered as much as I thought I did?

That's when I realized this was more than FOMO.

FOMO says, I wish I had been there.

This was deeper.

This said, I wish they had wanted me there.

And if you let that thought sit long enough, another one isn't far behind:

Maybe I'm just replaceable.

If I'm being vulnerable, that fear goes deeper than one season or one friendship.

I've often been the one who reaches out.

The one who sends the text.
The one who asks if everyone wants to get together.
The one who remembers the birthday.
The one who checks in after the hard week.
The one who notices when someone gets quiet.
The one who tries to keep everyone connected.

I became so good at pursuing people that I forgot what it felt like to be pursued.

And eventually you start wondering what would happen if you stopped.

If I didn't send the text, would one come?
If I didn't make the plans, would anyone make plans with me?
If I stopped checking in, how long would it take someone to check on me?
Would anyone notice?

Those questions exposed something in my heart I hadn't really wanted to admit.

I wasn't only craving friendship.

I was looking to friendship for reassurance that I mattered.

And Scripture doesn't tell us that relationships aren't important. Quite the opposite.

God Himself said, “It is not good for the man to be alone” in Genesis 2:18.

Ecclesiastes tells us that two are better than one and that when one falls, another can help them up.

Paul repeatedly describes believers as members of one body who need one another.

We were created for community.

So wanting to be known, remembered, and loved isn't weakness.

God created us for community—but He never created community to be our identity.

That's where I had gotten things twisted.

I like loving people intentionally.

I like remembering the details.

I like checking in.

I like bringing people together.

I believe those things reflect something beautiful about the way God calls us to love one another.

Galatians 6:2 tells us to carry one another's burdens.

Romans 12:10 tells us to be devoted to one another in love.

Philippians 2:4 tells us not to look only to our own interests, but also to the interests of others.

Intentional friendship is biblical.

But I've learned there is a loneliness that can come with being the person who tries to do those things for everyone else.

The people who build community often go home wondering why no one creates it for them.

And somewhere along the way, I let that loneliness tell me things about myself that God never did.

You're forgettable.

You're replaceable.

People eventually find someone they like better.

You're worth loving—as long as you're the one doing the work.

Those thoughts felt true.

But feeling true and being true are two very different things.

Second Corinthians 10:5 tells us to “take captive every thought to make it obedient to Christ.”

That means I don't get to accept every thought simply because it came from somewhere deep inside me.

I have to hold it against what God has already said.

And that's where this stopped being only about friendship.

Because pain has a way of becoming a terrible theologian.

If we don't surrender our hurt to God, our hurt will start interpreting God for us.

I had taken the actions—or inactions—of people and allowed them to tell me who I was.

I was measuring my worth by invitations.
My belonging by group pictures.
My importance by who texted first.
My value by whether someone noticed when I wasn't there.

But Scripture never tells me to find my identity in how well other people love me.

It tells me to find it in how completely God already has.

Before I was ever included by people, I was chosen by God.

“For he chose us in him before the creation of the world to be holy and blameless in his sight.”
— Ephesians 1:4 (NIV)

I didn't become chosen when someone picked me as their best friend.

I was chosen in Christ before I had ever taken a breath.

Before someone thought to invite me, God had already given me a place to belong.

“Consequently, you are no longer foreigners and strangers, but fellow citizens with God's people and also members of his household.”
— Ephesians 2:19 (NIV)

Not tolerated.

Not squeezed in.

Not invited because someone else canceled.

A member of His household.

Before my name crossed someone else's mind, I was already completely known by Him.

Psalm 139 says He knows when I sit and when I rise. He perceives my thoughts from afar. He is familiar with all my ways.

There has never been a moment when God looked around and suddenly realized I wasn't there.

Isaiah 49 gives an even more beautiful picture.

God's people wondered whether He had forgotten them.

His answer?

“Can a mother forget the baby at her breast and have no compassion on the child she has borne? Though she may forget, I will not forget you!”
— Isaiah 49:15 (NIV)

Then He says:

“See, I have engraved you on the palms of my hands.”
— Isaiah 49:16 (NIV)

That verse hits differently when you've spent a season wondering whether anyone remembers you.

I may be absent from someone's plans, but I am never absent from God's mind.

And when the fear whispers, You're replaceable, Scripture answers that too.

“But you are a chosen people, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, God's special possession...”
— 1 Peter 2:9 (NIV)

His possession.
His child.
His workmanship.
His beloved.

Ephesians 2:10 says we are God's handiwork, created in Christ Jesus for good works He prepared in advance for us to do.

God didn't mass-produce me and hope I'd find somewhere to fit.

He created me intentionally.

You cannot be replaced in a purpose God specifically prepared for you.

And before anyone chose to pursue friendship with me, Jesus had already pursued me.

“But God demonstrates his own love for us in this: While we were still sinners, Christ died for us.”
— Romans 5:8 (NIV)

He didn't wait for me to reach out first.

He didn't wait until I became easy to love.

He didn't love me because I was convenient.

He didn't choose me because I had something useful to offer Him.

He came for me.

The gospel is the story of a God who moves toward people who could never get to Him on their own.

The cross settled my worth long before my friendships ever questioned it.

And Jesus knows something about being left too.

He was misunderstood.

Rejected.

Abandoned.

Isaiah described Him as “despised and rejected by mankind” in Isaiah 53:3.

In His greatest hour of suffering, His closest friends scattered.

So when I bring Him the ache of rejection, I'm not bringing it to a Savior who can't understand.

Hebrews 4:15 reminds us that we don't have a high priest who is unable to empathize with our weaknesses.

Jesus doesn't stand at a distance from rejection. He knows what it is to be left.

That doesn't mean friendship loss suddenly stops hurting.

Biblical truth doesn't require me to pretend pain isn't painful.

I can miss people.
I can grieve what a friendship used to be.
I can wish someone had noticed.
I can acknowledge that some relationships weren't as mutual as I believed they were.
I can even tell God, This really hurt me.

The Psalms are full of people doing exactly that.

God isn't intimidated by honest grief.

But I don't have to turn grief into identity.

Someone forgetting me doesn't make me forgettable.
Someone not choosing me doesn't make me unchosen.
Someone growing closer to somebody else doesn't make me replaceable.
Having another baby didn't make me less worthy of being pursued.

My circumstances were speaking loudly, but Scripture had already spoken first.

Today, my friendships look different.

God has brought new people into my life, and I'm learning to receive those friendships without constantly waiting for the moment I'm replaced again.

That's still a work in progress.

Because when you've experienced the same hurt more than once, it's tempting to expect every new relationship to eventually end the same way.

But fear isn't supposed to dictate how I love.

“There is no fear in love. But perfect love drives out fear...”
— 1 John 4:18 (NIV)

I don't want yesterday's rejection to make me suspicious of today's gift.

I don't want to hold people at arm's length because someone else let me down.

I don't want to love halfway because I'm afraid someone will eventually leave.

And I definitely don't want another person's ability—or inability—to love me well to determine what I believe about myself.

So I'm learning.

I'm learning that I can love deeply without making people responsible for my identity.

I'm learning that I can treasure friendship without worshiping acceptance.

I'm learning that some people may only walk beside me for a season, and their leaving doesn't erase what God did while they were there.

I'm learning to appreciate the people God has placed in front of me instead of constantly looking over my shoulder wondering when they'll leave.

I'm learning that being forgotten by people can never undo the fact that I am fully known by God.

And I'm learning something else.

God Himself is not a consolation prize for the friendships I wish had worked out differently.

He is the treasure.

“Whom have I in heaven but you? And earth has nothing I desire besides you.”
— Psalm 73:25 (NIV)

That's where I want my heart to land.

Not at, I don't need people.

I do.

God made us for relationship.

But people were never supposed to answer the deepest questions of my soul.

Am I wanted?

Do I belong?

Am I worth pursuing?

Can I be replaced?

Those questions have already been answered in Christ.

Maybe you've experienced this too.

Maybe you've watched your friendships change as your family grew.

Maybe you've stared at a picture from something you weren't invited to and felt that familiar sting.

Maybe you're always the one sending the first text.

Maybe you've wondered when exactly you stopped being someone people thought to include.

And maybe you've told yourself the same lie I have:

Maybe I'm just replaceable.

Take that thought back to Scripture.

Because our feelings deserve to be acknowledged, but they don't get the final word.

God does.

FOMO says, I'm missing out.

Fear says, I'm being replaced.

But Scripture says:

I am chosen. — Ephesians 1:4
I belong. — Ephesians 2:19
I am fully known. — Psalm 139
I am not forgotten. — Isaiah 49:15–16
I am God's workmanship. — Ephesians 2:10
I am His. — 1 Peter 2:9

None of those truths change when the invitation doesn't come.

Maybe the invitation I've been longing for isn't ultimately another girls' night.

Maybe it's the invitation to stop asking people to answer a question God already settled at the cross.

Friendship is a gift, but it was never meant to carry the weight of my identity.

There is a table where my seat has never been uncertain.

And Scripture gives us that picture too.

David writes of God preparing a table before him.

Jesus repeatedly used the picture of a feast and a kingdom invitation.

And Revelation points us toward the wedding supper of the Lamb.

The whole story of Scripture is moving toward a people who belong completely to God.

So when an earthly table has an empty chair where I thought mine would be, I can grieve it.

But I don't have to question whether I belong.

Because there is a place where I don't have to wonder whether I'm still wanted.

A place where I don't have to earn belonging by being useful, available, funny, easy, or the one who always reaches out first.

Jesus has already made the way for me.

And maybe that's the truth my heart needs most when another invitation doesn't come:

The table I feared I'd lost my seat at was never the table that defined me.

People may forget to invite me.

People may stop reaching out.

Friendships may change.

But the God who engraved His people on the palms of His hands has not forgotten me.

The invitations from people may come and go. His never has.
