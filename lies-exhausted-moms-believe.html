The Lie Exhausted Moms Believe

July 2026

I do not think the hardest part of motherhood is always the sleepless nights. Or the laundry. Or even the noise.

Sometimes the hardest part is believing you are supposed to have enough of yourself for everyone.

Enough patience.

Enough gentleness.

Enough energy.

Enough affection.

Enough wisdom.

Enough grace.

By the end of the day, there is nothing left.

The kids have climbed into your lap a hundred times. Someone has needed a snack, a diaper, a hug, a correction, a clean shirt, a found shoe, a listening ear, or the answer to a question they have already asked twelve times.

Then your husband reaches for your hand. Or wraps his arms around you. And before you even think, you pull away.

Not because you do not love him.

Not because you do not want your marriage.

Not because affection is wrong.

But because your body and mind feel like they have been claimed by everyone else all day.

“The hardest part isn't that everyone needs me. It's that by the end of the day, I don't know where everyone else ends and I begin.”

That is what people often mean when they say they are touched out.

It is not always about not wanting affection.

Sometimes it is about forgetting what it feels like to belong to yourself for five quiet minutes.

“I spend all day giving pieces of myself away, then wonder why I have nothing left to offer the person I love most.”

That is a painful thing to admit.

Because the people we love most often meet the version of us that everyone else has already exhausted.

Sometimes the person we push away is not the one who drained us.

He is just the one who arrived after we were already empty.

And in marriage, that can become dangerous ground.

Because one person feels depleted.

The other feels rejected.

One person feels overwhelmed.

The other feels unwanted.

And the enemy does not have to destroy a marriage if he can simply keep two exhausted people misunderstanding each other.

“Sometimes what feels like rejection is actually depletion.”

That does not mean exhaustion excuses coldness.

It does not mean being touched out gives permission to be careless with someone's heart.

But it does mean we need more than assumptions.

We need honesty.

Love is not always measured by how much energy you have left.

Sometimes it is choosing honesty instead of pretending you are okay.

Intimacy begins long before physical touch.

It begins where honesty replaces assumption.

But there is also a deeper question underneath the exhaustion.

Why do we keep believing we have to be enough?

Somewhere along the way, many women start believing that a good wife always has more to give.

A good mom never runs empty.

A faithful Christian serves without ever feeling depleted.

But that is not the gospel.

“Come to me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest.”
— Matthew 11:28 (NIV)

Jesus did not call the weary to try harder.

He called them to come closer.

“The gospel never asks exhausted people to become stronger. It invites weary people to come to a stronger Savior.”

Rest is not something you earn after you have finished everything.

It is something God gives to people who admit they never will.

That is the invitation of Christ.

Not “come when you are impressive.”

Not “come when you have held everything together.”

Not “come when you are no longer overwhelmed.”

Come weary.

Come burdened.

Come empty.

Come honest.

Motherhood has a way of convincing women that being needed is the same thing as being faithful.

But they are not always the same.

“Children need a mother who knows Jesus more than they need a mother who never gets tired.”

You can spend all day meeting needs and still neglect the one soul God entrusted to your care—your own.

You can become so constantly available to everyone else that you slowly become unavailable to God.

And when that happens, even good things can become crushing things.

Laundry becomes heavier.

Noise becomes louder.

Affection feels like another demand.

Small requests feel impossible.

Not because your family is the enemy.

But because you were never created to be the source.

“God never asked you to become everyone's source.

He only asked you to stay connected to yours.”

Jesus said it this way:

“I am the vine; you are the branches. If you remain in me and I in you, you will bear much fruit; apart from me you can do nothing.”
— John 15:5 (NIV)

That verse is both comforting and confronting.

Apart from Him, we can do nothing.

Not less.

Not barely enough.

Nothing.

Yet so often, we try to produce patience, gentleness, peace, self-control, and love from an empty soul.

But Scripture calls those things fruit of the Spirit.

“But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, forbearance, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness and self-control.”
— Galatians 5:22-23 (NIV)

Fruit does not grow because a branch tries harder.

Fruit grows because the branch stays connected.

“You were never created to be the vine.

Branches don't survive by trying harder.

They survive by remaining.”

That may be the truth many exhausted women need most.

Not another system.

Not another standard.

Not another reason to feel guilty.

But an invitation to remain.

To abide.

To stop trying to be the Savior of the home when the home already has one.

Because sometimes the greatest threat to peace is not the schedule.

Sometimes it is the belief that everything depends on us.

“Maybe I'm not overwhelmed because my family expects too much. Maybe I'm overwhelmed because I keep trying to be what only God can be.”

That is where conviction begins.

Not shame.

Conviction.

Shame says, “You are failing.”

Conviction says, “Come back to the Father.”

Shame says, “Try harder.”

Conviction says, “Remain in Christ.”

Shame says, “You should be enough by now.”

The gospel says, “You were never meant to be.”

“I'm quick to confess impatience. I'm slower to confess self-sufficiency.”

It is easy to confess the moment we snapped.

It is harder to confess the pride underneath it.

The belief that we are indispensable.

The belief that if we stop holding everything together, everything will fall apart.

The belief that everyone's peace depends on our performance.

“I don't just need a nap. I need repentance for believing I'm indispensable.”

Sometimes exhaustion reveals more than our need for rest.

Sometimes exhaustion reveals what we have been worshiping.

Control.

Being needed.

Being capable.

Being enough.

But the cross does not say, “You can handle this.”

It says, “You never could. That is why Jesus came.”

“The pressure to be enough doesn't come from God. It comes from forgetting that Christ already is.”

Christ is enough when the house is loud.

Christ is enough when your body feels overstimulated.

Christ is enough when marriage feels tender and misunderstood.

Christ is enough when you realize you have been trying to pour from an empty place.

Christ is enough when your peace disappears the moment someone needs you again.

And if peace disappears every time someone needs us, maybe our peace was never rooted in Christ to begin with.

That is not a comfortable truth.

But it is a freeing one.

Because if the problem is deeper than exhaustion, then the answer is deeper than a nap.

Yes, rest matters.

Sleep matters.

Help matters.

Communication matters.

Boundaries matter.

But none of those things can replace abiding in Christ.

“Some days I think I'm touched out.

But maybe what I really am...

is living like I'm everyone's source instead of remembering who mine is.”

So maybe the prayer is not only, “Lord, give me more energy.”

Maybe the prayer is, “Lord, teach me dependence.”

Maybe the prayer is not only, “Help me get through the day.”

Maybe it is, “Help me stop living like I am the one holding everything together.”

Maybe it is not only, “Make me a better wife and mother.”

Maybe it is, “Make me a woman who remains in You.”

“When I believe everyone needs me, I've forgotten that everyone—including me—needs Jesus more.”

There will still be laundry tomorrow.

There will still be little hands.

There will still be questions.

There will still be noise.

There will still be moments in marriage that require tenderness, honesty, and humility.

But there will also be mercy.
