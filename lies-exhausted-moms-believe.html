The Lie Exhausted Moms Believe

July 2026

“Come to me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest.”
Matthew 11:28 (NIV)

I used to think the hardest part of motherhood was simply being tired. I blamed the sleepless nights, the endless laundry, the dishes that somehow multiplied while I wasn't looking, and the constant interruptions that made even the simplest task feel impossible. Surely that was the problem. Surely if I could just get one uninterrupted nap, one quiet afternoon, or one hot cup of coffee from beginning to end, I'd finally feel like myself again.

But the longer I've been a mom, the more I've realized exhaustion has a way of exposing something much deeper than our need for sleep. It exposes what we believe. Somewhere between packing lunches, wiping tears, changing diapers, answering endless questions, and carrying the invisible weight that comes with loving tiny humans, I quietly started believing things that sounded true but weren't. They didn't feel like lies. They felt responsible. They felt loving. They even felt spiritual. That's what makes them so dangerous.

"The enemy rarely convinces exhausted moms to stop loving their families. He simply convinces them they were never meant to be human in the first place."

The biggest lie settled into my heart so gradually I never noticed it had become the soundtrack of my days. If I were a good mom, I would always have enough. Enough patience for one more question. Enough gentleness after correcting the same behavior for the tenth time. Enough energy to make another meal, fold another basket of laundry, wipe another face, referee another argument, and still smile when everyone needed something else before I could finally sit down. I never actually said those words out loud, but I lived as though they were true.

Every morning I woke up determined to pour myself out for everyone around me. Every evening I wondered why I felt so empty.

Maybe you've been there too. By the end of the day, the kids have climbed into your lap a hundred times. Someone needed a snack. Someone couldn't find their favorite stuffed animal. Someone spilled their milk across the table five minutes after you cleaned it. Someone needed help with homework while another needed a diaper changed. The baby cried because you walked into the other room for thirty seconds. You reheated your coffee twice before finally giving up and drinking it cold. None of those moments seem significant by themselves, but together they quietly become the story of your day. You have spent hours giving away tiny pieces of yourself until there's almost nothing left.

Then your husband reaches for your hand.

Or wraps his arms around you while you're standing at the kitchen sink.

Before you've even had time to think about it, you instinctively pull away. Not because you don't love him. Not because your marriage doesn't matter. Not because affection is wrong. But because your body and your mind feel like they've belonged to everyone else all day.

"Sometimes what feels like rejection is actually depletion."

People often call this being touched out, and I think that's real. There are seasons when every hug, every little hand, every voice calling, "Mom!" leaves you feeling like there's nothing left to give. But I wonder if that's only part of the story. As I sat with the Lord one morning, I sensed Him gently asking a question I hadn't considered before.

Why do you believe you have to be enough?

That question stopped me. I had been asking God for more patience, more energy, more peace, more self-control, and more joy. But I hadn't stopped to ask why I believed all of those things had to come from me in the first place.

Looking back, I realized I had quietly started living as though I were the source. The source of peace in our home. The source of everyone's happiness. The source of stability. The source of patience. The source of comfort. The source of everything my family needed. No wonder I was exhausted. I was carrying a responsibility God had never asked me to carry.

"The pressure to be enough doesn't come from God. It comes from forgetting that Christ already is."

That's why Jesus' words in John 15 have become so precious to me. I've read them countless times, but motherhood has made me understand them in a way I never could before.

“I am the vine; you are the branches. If you remain in me and I in you, you will bear much fruit; apart from me you can do nothing.”
John 15:5 (NIV)

Notice what Jesus doesn't say. He doesn't tell the branches to try harder. He doesn't tell them to produce more fruit through greater effort. He doesn't tell them to become stronger or more capable. He simply tells them to remain. That's such a simple word, but it confronts nearly everything our culture tells mothers. We celebrate the women who do it all, carry it all, fix it all, and somehow keep smiling through it all. Somewhere along the way, we've confused being needed with being faithful.

But being constantly needed isn't the same thing as remaining connected to Christ. It's possible to spend so much time meeting everyone else's needs that we slowly neglect the only relationship capable of sustaining us. We become so available to everyone else that we become unavailable to God, and then we wonder why we're running on empty.

"Children need a mother who knows Jesus more than they need a mother who never gets tired."

I wonder how many of us have mistaken constant availability for godliness. We feel guilty sitting down. Guilty saying no. Guilty asking for help. Guilty admitting we're overwhelmed. Somehow we've come to believe faithful women should never run empty.

But Jesus never praised exhaustion.

He invited the weary to come to Him. "The invitation of Jesus has never been, 'Hold everything together.' It's always been, 'Come to Me.'"

That verse from Matthew 11 has taken on a completely different meaning since becoming a mom. Before, I thought Jesus was talking about the obvious burdens—the tragedies, the grief, the heartbreak that brings you to your knees. But now I wonder if He also had ordinary Tuesday afternoons in mind. The afternoons when you're folding laundry with one hand while holding a toddler with the other. The evenings when dinner is burning, someone is crying, and you realize you haven't sat down all day. The moments when you're so overstimulated that one more person saying your name feels like more than you can handle.

Jesus knew we would become weary. He wasn't surprised by it, and He certainly wasn't disappointed by it. That's why His invitation wasn't, "Come to Me once you've finally figured this out." It wasn't, "Come to Me after you've become more patient." It wasn't even, "Come to Me after you've learned how to balance everything." His invitation was simply, "Come to Me, all you who are weary and burdened." He knew weariness would find us. He just never intended for it to become our home.

I wonder how many of us have confused striving with faithfulness. We keep telling ourselves we're doing all of this for our families, but if we're honest, there's often another voice underneath it all. It's the voice that says a good mom never drops the ball. A good wife always has more to give. A faithful Christian shouldn't feel overwhelmed. We don't just want to love our families well—we want to prove we're enough for them.

"The lie isn't that your family needs you. The lie is that they need you more than they need Jesus."

That sentence stings a little because it exposes something deeper than exhaustion. It exposes self-sufficiency. Not the loud kind that boasts about independence, but the quiet kind that believes everything rests on our shoulders. If I don't remember the appointments, who will? If I don't keep the peace, who will? If I don't meet everyone's needs, everything will fall apart.

But that's a heavy burden for someone who was never meant to be the Savior.

Sometimes I think my exhaustion isn't simply revealing how much I've been doing. It's revealing how much I've been trying to control. I want everyone to be okay. I want my children to feel secure. I want my husband to feel loved. I want our home to feel peaceful. None of those desires are wrong, but somewhere along the way they quietly became responsibilities I believed belonged to me alone.

The more I read Scripture, the more I realize God has never asked me to carry the weight of outcomes. He's asked me to be faithful. Those are two very different things. Faithfulness belongs to me. The results belong to Him.

When Jesus said, "Apart from Me you can do nothing," I used to hear that verse as a warning. Now I hear it as a relief. I don't have to squeeze patience out of an empty heart. I don't have to manufacture joy after a sleepless night. I don't have to pretend peace when my soul is anxious. If those things are truly the fruit of the Spirit, then they were never meant to grow from my own strength in the first place.

"Fruit doesn't grow because the branch works harder. It grows because the branch stays connected."

I think that's why so many of us end our days feeling defeated. We've spent the entire day trying to produce what only God can provide. We ask ourselves why we're so impatient, but we rarely ask whether we've remained close to Christ. We confess our snapping, our frustration, and our short tempers, but we're slower to confess the pride underneath them—the quiet belief that we can somehow do this on our own.

I'm quick to confess impatience.

I'm slower to confess self-sufficiency.

Because self-sufficiency feels respectable. It looks responsible. People even compliment it. They tell us they're amazed by how much we juggle. They call us strong. Capable. Supermom.

But the gospel has never invited us to become impressive.

It invites us to become dependent.

That's why Paul could write, "For when I am weak, then I am strong." He wasn't celebrating weakness itself. He was celebrating what weakness forced him to do—depend on Christ instead of himself. Maybe that's why God doesn't always remove our weariness as quickly as we'd like. Sometimes the very thing we're begging Him to take away is the thing drawing us closer to His heart.

I don't think God wastes exhaustion.

I think He uses it to gently expose every place we've started trusting ourselves more than Him. Not to shame us, but to invite us back. Conviction has never been God's way of pushing His children away. It's His loving hand on our shoulder saying, "Come closer. You're carrying something I never asked you to carry."

"Conviction says, 'Come back to the Father.' Shame says, 'Figure it out yourself.'"

Maybe today you're reading this with dishes in the sink, laundry in the dryer, and toys scattered across your living room. Maybe someone interrupted you three times before you finished this paragraph. Maybe you're already thinking about everything waiting for you when you put your phone down.

Friend, those things will probably still be there in an hour.

The laundry will still need folding.

The dishes will still need washing.

Your children will still ask for another snack.

Life isn't suddenly going to become quiet.

But your soul doesn't have to keep carrying what Christ has already carried for you.

The greatest gift you can give your family isn't becoming a woman who never gets tired. It's becoming a woman who knows where to go when she does. Your children don't need a mother who has all the answers. They need a mother who shows them what it looks like to depend on Jesus. Your husband doesn't need a wife who never struggles. He needs a wife who knows that Christ is enough for both of you.

So maybe today's prayer isn't, "Lord, help me hold everything together."

Maybe it's, "Lord, forgive me for believing I ever could."

Because the greatest lie exhausted moms believe isn't that they're tired.

It's that everything depends on them.

The gospel gently reminds us that it never has.

Christ is enough.

He always has been.

He always will be.
